The shop opens at eight in the morning and closes at six in the evening. The first job of the day is to check the repair board. The repair board lists every bike in the shop, the work it needs, and the day the customer will pick it up. A bike that is due today goes to the front of the line. A bike that is waiting for a part goes to the back of the line, and the customer gets a call.

Most of the bikes that come into the shop need one of four repairs. They need a new tube, a brake adjustment, a gear adjustment, or a clean chain. A flat tire is the most common repair. A brake adjustment is the second most common repair. The mechanic checks the whole bike before starting any repair, because a bike that comes in for one problem often has another.

To fix a flat tire, the mechanic takes the wheel off the bike. The mechanic lets the rest of the air out of the tube and pushes the tire away from the rim. The mechanic slides a tire lever under the edge of the tire and lifts the edge over the rim. The mechanic pulls the old tube out of the tire. Then the mechanic runs a hand slowly around the inside of the tire, because a small piece of glass or wire can stay in the tire and cause the next flat. If the mechanic finds the cause, the mechanic takes it out. If the mechanic does not find the cause, the mechanic checks the rim tape, because a sharp edge on the rim can also cut the tube.

The mechanic puts a little air in the new tube, so that it holds its shape. The mechanic puts the valve through the hole in the rim and tucks the tube into the tire. The mechanic pushes the edge of the tire back over the rim with both thumbs, working from the valve around the wheel. The mechanic does not use a tire lever for the last part, because a lever can pinch the new tube. The mechanic pumps the tire to the pressure that is printed on the side of the tire. Then the mechanic puts the wheel back on the bike and spins it, to check that the tire sits evenly on the rim.

To adjust the brakes, the mechanic squeezes the brake lever and watches the brake pads. The pads should touch the rim at the same time, and they should touch the rim and not the tire. If a pad touches the tire, it can wear a hole in the tire. If the lever moves too far before the brakes work, the cable is too loose. The mechanic turns the barrel adjuster to tighten the cable a little at a time. The mechanic squeezes the lever after each turn. When the brakes work before the lever is halfway to the handlebar, the brakes are ready. If the pads are worn down to the line, the mechanic puts in new pads.

To adjust the gears, the mechanic puts the bike in the stand and turns the pedals by hand. The mechanic shifts through every gear, one at a time, and listens to the chain. A chain that rattles or skips is a chain that needs an adjustment. The mechanic turns the barrel adjuster on the derailleur a quarter turn at a time, and turns the pedals again after each turn. When the chain moves to each gear on the first try and runs without noise, the gears are ready. If the gears still skip after an adjustment, the chain or the cassette may be worn, and the mechanic measures the chain with a chain checker.

To clean a chain, the mechanic puts the bike in the stand and holds a rag around the chain. The mechanic turns the pedals backward, so that the chain runs through the rag. The mechanic puts a little degreaser on a brush and scrubs the chain, the cassette, and the front rings. Then the mechanic wipes the chain dry with a clean rag. The mechanic puts one drop of oil on each link of the chain and turns the pedals backward again. Last, the mechanic wipes the extra oil off the chain, because oil on the outside of the chain picks up dirt. A clean chain is quiet, and a quiet chain lasts longer.

When a customer brings a bike into the shop, the person at the front desk writes a repair ticket. The ticket has the customer's name, the bike, the problem, and the day the customer needs the bike back. The person at the front desk asks the customer to describe the problem in their own words. A customer may say that the bike makes a noise, that the brakes feel soft, or that the chain falls off. The person at the front desk writes down what the customer says, and the mechanic works out the cause.

The shop gives every customer a price before it starts the work. If the mechanic finds another problem during the repair, the mechanic stops and the shop calls the customer. The shop never does extra work without asking the customer first. If the customer says yes, the mechanic does the extra work. If the customer says no, the mechanic finishes the first repair and writes the other problem on the ticket.

When a repair is done, the mechanic rides the bike in the parking lot. The mechanic checks the brakes, shifts through the gears, and listens for noise. If the bike rides well, the mechanic puts a tag on the bike and moves the bike to the pickup rack. The person at the front desk calls the customer to say that the bike is ready. If the bike does not ride well, the mechanic puts it back in the stand and finds the problem.

The shop sells tubes, tires, brake pads, chains, lights, locks, and helmets. The shop does not sell new bikes. The shop sells a few used bikes in the spring, after the mechanics check them and fix them. Every used bike leaves the shop with new brake pads, a clean chain, and tires that hold air.

On a busy day, the shop fixes twenty bikes. On a slow day, the shop fixes eight or nine bikes, and the mechanics clean the tools and sort the parts. The mechanics keep the tools on the wall above each stand, so that every tool has a place. A tool that is not in its place is a tool that someone will look for later.

In the spring, more bikes come into the shop than at any other time of the year. People take their bikes out of the garage after the winter and find flat tires, rusty chains, and brakes that do not work. In the summer, the shop is busy with flat tires and broken spokes. In the fall, people bring their bikes in for a check before the rain starts. In the winter, the shop is quiet, and the mechanics build wheels and rebuild old bikes for the spring.

The shop teaches a repair class on the first Saturday of every month. In the class, each person learns to fix a flat tire, to adjust the brakes, and to clean a chain. Each person brings their own bike to the class. The mechanic shows each step on a shop bike, and then each person does the same step on their own bike. At the end of the class, each person can fix a flat tire on the side of the road.

The mechanics write notes about every repair on the back of the repair ticket. A good note says what the mechanic found, what the mechanic did, and what the customer should watch for. A note that says only "fixed" does not help the next mechanic. A note that says "new tube, glass in the tire, tire is worn, replace it soon" helps the next mechanic and the customer.

The shop keeps a list of the questions that customers ask most often. How often should I pump my tires? Check the pressure once a week, because a tube loses a little air every day. How often should I clean my chain? Clean it once a month, or after any ride in the rain. How do I know when my brake pads are worn? Look at the line on the pad, and replace the pad when the line is gone. Why does my chain skip? The gears may need an adjustment, or the chain may be worn. How long does a repair take? Most repairs take one day, and a repair that needs a part takes as long as the part takes to arrive.

At the end of the day, the mechanics put the tools back on the wall and sweep the floor. The person at the front desk checks the repair board and moves the tickets for tomorrow to the top. The last person to leave turns off the lights, locks the door, and checks that the bikes on the pickup rack are locked to the rack. Then the shop is closed until eight the next morning.
